         Parametric Feedback Control for Shaping Stationary Probability Density Using
                             Physics-Informed Neural Networks

                                         Wantao Jiaa,b , Xianzheng Lia,b , Xiaotong Fenga,b,∗

           b MOE Key Laboratory for Complexity Science in Aerospace, Northwestern Polytechnical University, Xi’an, 710072, China




     Abstract
     This paper develops a physics-informed neural network framework for feedback control of the stationary probability
     density of stochastic dynamical systems. The objective is to obtain approximate density-matching feedback controls
     for prescribed stationary target densities under the Fokker–Planck–Kolmogorov constraints. It is assumed that the
     considered closed-loop systems admit suitable stationary distributions at fixed target-parameter values. The framework
     uses separate density and control networks to construct a parameter-conditioned approximation, and compares three
     FPK-residual treatment strategies under a common base training configuration and strategy-specific hyperparameter
     settings. The density tracking problem is formulated as a constrained optimization problem, and three constraint
     handling strategies are investigated: the soft constraint method, penalty method, and barrier method. The neural
     networks take both state variables and target distribution parameters as inputs, enabling parametric predictions of
     stationary probability density functions (PDFs) and their associated feedback controls within prescribed parameter
     ranges without retraining. Under the adopted training and simulation settings, the barrier method achieves the
     lowest reported closed-loop Monte Carlo (MC) L1 density errors for both Single-degree-of-freedom (SDOF) targets and
     both levitation targets, demonstrating an accuracy advantage in these two-dimensional cases. The method rankings
     vary across the Duffing marginals, while the first SDOF benchmark shows a higher training cost for the barrier
     method. Independent MC simulations are further conducted by substituting the learned feedback controls into the
     original stochastic systems. Under the simulated initial conditions and numerical settings, the long-time MC response
     densities reproduce the principal distributional characteristics of the prescribed targets at representative parameter
     values. These results do not establish uniqueness of the stationary distribution or convergence from arbitrary initial
     distributions. Overall, the proposed framework provides a unified approach for parametric stationary probability
     density control without requiring a prescribed analytical feedback law, and the numerical results demonstrate its
     capabilities in parametric approximation, constraint handling, and closed-loop response prediction.
     Keywords: Physics-informed neural networks, Stochastic dynamical systems, Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov equation,
     Parametric Feedback control, Probability density tracking, Deep learning


1    1. Introduction

2        In many scientific and industrial applications, it is necessary to regulate the dynamical behavior of a system in order
3    to meet performance and safety requirements. For stochastic dynamical systems[1, 2], traditional control objectives are
4    often formulated in terms of statistical moments of the system response, such as the mean, variance, and covariance[3, 4].
5    These low-order statistics provide useful information but cannot fully characterize the stochastic response, particularly
6    when the response exhibits non-Gaussian features, multi-modal distributions, or pronounced tail behavior [5, 6]. This
7    limitation motivates stochastic tracking control, in which the stationary response probability density is taken as the
8    control objective. Compared with moment control, density tracking control is more challenging because the control
9    law must shape an entire distribution while preserving the feasibility requirements of a probability density function
10   (PDF).

11       The concept of PDF shaping control has attracted considerable attention in the control community over the past two
12   decades. Early work by Wang [7] established a B-spline-based framework for controlling the output PDF in discrete
13   time systems, which was subsequently extended to multivariate systems [8] and bounded control scenarios [9, 10].
14   For continuous time stochastic systems governed by stochastic differential equations (SDEs), the stationary PDF
15   is characterized by the Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov (FPK) equation [11, 12]. Annunziato and Borzì [13] formulated
16   the PDF control problem as a bilinear optimal control problem governed by the FPK equation. On the analytical
17   side, Zhu et al. [14, 15] designed tracking control for multi-degree-of-freedom nonlinear systems by exploiting exact
18   stationary solutions, while Yang et al. [16] developed a Hamiltonian-based feedback control strategy using polynomial
19   expansions and stochastic averaging. Stochastic averaging techniques have also been extended to systems under
20   combined Gaussian and Poisson white noise excitations [17, 18], broadening the applicability of analytical methods to
21   non-Gaussian stochastic environments. Alongside classical formulations, probabilistic target tracking has recently been
22   investigated for practical systems under diffusion and jump disturbances [19], while stationary PDF tracking control
23   has been developed for stochastic levitation systems [20].
24       These developments motivate consideration of analytical applicability, numerical dimensionality, and reuse across
25   target-parameter values. First, analytical approaches that yield closed-form control expressions rely on restrictive
26   solvability conditions and are therefore applicable only to systems with specific structures [21–23]. Second, numerical
27   methods for the FPK equation, such as finite element methods[24, 25], finite difference methods[26], spectral methods
28   [27], and path integral methods [28–30], can alleviate the limitations imposed by analytical solvability conditions to
29   some extent. However, they suffer from the curse of dimensionality [31] and typically require substantial computational
30   effort for each parameter configuration. Third, many applications require feedback controls for families of prescribed
31   target densities with varying widths, locations, or shapes. When a parameterized analytical control expression is
32   available, it can be evaluated directly at different parameter values, as illustrated by Eq.(25). For problems addressed
33   through numerical approximation, a parameter-conditioned model offers a reusable mapping from target-distribution
34   parameters to stationary PDFs and associated feedback controls. Once trained, it can be evaluated within the prescribed
35   parameter range without separate training or a new numerical solution for each target-parameter value.
36       In recent years, deep learning methods have shown promising potential for solving the FPK equation and approx-
37   imating response PDFs under prescribed dynamics in stochastic dynamical systems [32–35]. In particular, Physics-
38   Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) [36–41] provide a framework for solving forward and inverse problems by integrating
39   physical constraints into deep learning. Through automatic differentiation, PINNs incorporate governing equation and
40   boundary condition residuals into the training objective to approximate solutions to the underlying problems. Building
41   on these developments, stationary PDF tracking control can be formulated as an inverse design problem, in which the
42   feedback control is sought so that the controlled stationary density approaches a prescribed target under the stationary
43   FPK equation constraint. Related PINN-based studies provide a methodological basis for this formulation. Mowlavi
44   and Nabi [42] investigated partial differential equation(PDE) constrained optimal control using PINNs, while Lu et
45   al. [43] developed PINNs with hard constraints for inverse design, including PDE constrained topology optimiza-
46   tion. In addition, neural network based equation discovery has explored constrained optimization as an alternative
47   to unconstrained formulations [44], highlighting the importance of constraint treatment in physics-informed learning.
48   Although these studies address different optimization objectives and inverse problems, they provide useful insights into
49   incorporating physical constraints into neural network based inverse design for stationary density tracking control.
50       More directly related to this problem, Jia et al. [45] developed a neural network based approach for stationary
51   PDF tracking control by incorporating conditions associated with asymptotic stability in distribution, the stationary
52   FPK, detailed balance, and controller uniqueness into a weighted training objective. Their multi-output network
53   approximates the reversible and irreversible components of the control, with the prescribed target density directly
54   incorporated into the physical residuals. The proposed framework takes state variables as network inputs and considers
55   fixed target distributions in its numerical examples, without demonstrating the use of a single trained model for varying
56   target-distribution parameters within a prescribed range. Based on these developments, the present study focuses on
57   two questions: how to reuse trained models as target-distribution parameters vary, and how different treatments of the
58   governing-equation residual affect stationary density approximation and closed-loop stationary density matching.
59       The main contributions of this paper are as follows:

60     1. We construct a parameter-conditioned approximation of stationary density–control pairs by including state vari-
61        ables and target-distribution parameters as inputs to separate density and control networks. The displayed

118   Remark 1. The optimization problem Eq.(9) seeks a control mapping u(x, θ) over the prescribed target-parameter
119   range. Its neural approximation is trained jointly across sampled parameter values, rather than separately for each
120   fixed target.

121   Remark 2. The non-negativity of the learned PDF is imposed for all three training strategies through the exponential
122   output transformation of the PDF network. The soft, penalty, and barrier formulations considered in Section 3 therefore
123   differ in how the FPK residual is treated: the soft formulation uses a fixed residual weight, the penalty formulation
124   progressively increases the residual weight, and the barrier formulation combines an auxiliary relation with a clipped
125   logarithmic term. The barrier term is unrelated to the non-negativity of the PDF.


126   3. The physics-informed neural network framework

127       In this section, we present a physics-informed neural network (PINN) framework to solve the constrained optimiza-
128   tion problem Eq.(9). The key idea is to parameterize both the stationary PDF p(x, θ) and the control force u(x, θ)
129   using deep neural networks, and to train these networks by minimizing a loss function that encodes the objective
130   Eq.(9a) and the constraints Eq.(9b)–Eq.(9e). The original control problem is formulated as a constrained optimiza-
131   tion problem. In the PINN implementation, we solve a sampled unconstrained surrogate in which the FPK equation,
132   boundary condition, and normalization condition are incorporated through residual-based loss terms. Therefore, the
133   learned solution should be interpreted as an approximate solution under the adopted neural-network parameterization
134   and sampling strategy.

135   3.1. Neural network architecture
136       The prescribed target PDF ptarget (x, θ) specifies the desired distribution, whereas the stationary PDF p(x, θ)
137   represents the response induced by the feedback control. These two densities need not coincide, since an arbitrary
138   target may not be exactly attainable under the given dynamics and control structure. We therefore approximate the
139   response PDF and the feedback control with separate networks and optimize them jointly under the tracking objective
140   and the FPK, boundary, and normalization constraints. This formulation allows the learned response to balance
141   target tracking and dynamical consistency without assuming exact target attainability. For an analytically specified
142   attainable target, fixing the PDF and solving only for the control is also a valid alternative. We do not claim that
143   the two-network formulation is universally more accurate or computationally preferable. Moreover, small sampled loss
144   values alone do not establish that the learned PDF equals the stationary PDF induced by the learned control; this
145   consistency is further assessed through closed-loop MC simulations under the simulated initial conditions and numerical
146   settings. The validation tasks and their scope are summarized in Section 4.
147       To address the parametric control problem, we employ two fully connected deep neural networks. The first network,
148   denoted by pNN (x, θ; ωp ), approximates the stationary PDF p(x, θ), where ωp represents the trainable parameters
149   (weights and biases) of this network. The second network, denoted by uNN (x, θ; ωu ), approximates the control force
150   u(x, θ), with trainable parameters ωu . Both networks take as input the concatenated vector [x, θ], enabling the
151   networks to produce outputs that depend on both the state variables and the target-distribution parameters.
152       By including the target-distribution parameters θ as network inputs, the trained models can be evaluated at different
153   parameter values without separate training for each target. The displayed parameter sweeps assess this reuse.

198   3.2.1. Soft constraint method

427   5. Conclusion

428       The considered examples show that parameter-conditioned density and control networks can approximate the
429   principal features of prescribed stationary targets, including annular and asymmetric densities and four-dimensional
430   Duffing targets. The displayed density-network sweeps support reuse of the individual models at selected parameter
431   values within their training ranges. They do not establish the same parameter-conditioned control accuracy for all
432   three strategies, and the first Duffing sweep tests slice amplitude rather than the width along x4 . At representative
433   parameters, closed-loop MC simulations reproduce the main target concentration patterns. The relative integrated
434   density errors depend on the system and, for the Duffing examples, on the marginal being examined.
435       Under the adopted training and simulation settings, the barrier formulation offers an accuracy advantage over
436   the soft-constraint and penalty formulations in the reported two-dimensional closed-loop density comparisons. As
437   summarized in Table 4, it attains the lowest reported L1 errors for both SDOF targets and both levitation targets.
438   For the first levitation target, the error is 0.1100, compared with 0.4300 and 0.2820 for the soft-constraint and penalty
439   formulations, respectively. This provides numerical evidence of closer target-density agreement in that case. The
440   advantage is not uniform across all projections: the Duffing marginal rankings vary, and the first-target (x1 , x2 ) errors
441   are close.
442       Future work may examine full-joint and interval-wide validation, explicit physical input constraints, adaptive sam-
443   pling, and computational scaling. Extension to time-varying targets would require a separate transient formulation
444   and validation.
